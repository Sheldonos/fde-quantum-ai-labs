Every operating model has to earn its own existence. Ours begins by naming, plainly, where enterprise AI is going wrong today.
The Problem
Before defining anything we build, it is worth being precise about what is going wrong, because the whole design that follows is a response to it. The barrier to enterprise AI is no longer whether the technology is capable. It is everything that must be true for a working demonstration to become a dependable part of how a business operates.

~95%
of enterprise generative-AI initiatives deliver no measurable return, per widely cited 2025 research. The cause is seldom the model. It is the distance between a working concept and a system in daily use.
Where enterprise AI initiatives fail
01
Data and integration. Fragmented, siloed data and no real integration with existing ERP, CRM, or legacy systems. The pilot ran on curated data; production data is messy and governed.
02
No business alignment. A capability looking for a use case, not a named business constraint. No clear ROI metric, no executive sponsor.
03
Adoption and change. Resistance to workflow change and poor end-user adoption. The workflow was never adapted, so nothing actually changed.
04
Governance and compliance. Regulatory hurdles, data privacy, auditability, and bias or hallucination risk, with weak observability and trust.
05
Skill and context gaps. Remote or central teams that lose context, and not enough senior technical depth close to the problem.
06
Process and handoff. Siloed discovery, build, and deployment. Requirements documents that miss the real-world nuances that decide whether the system fits.
07
Scalability and maintenance. Fragile architectures, no reusable assets, no way to detect quality drift, and no clear owner once the builders leave.
These compound into the pilot-to-production gap, where an estimated 95 percent of enterprise AI stalls. Every one is organizational or procedural, not a failure of the model itself.
This is an industry-wide problem, but it is IBM's to solve. IBM sells the platforms these systems are built on, works inside the largest and most regulated enterprises where the gap is widest, and carries the credibility to be trusted with the data and workflows where the answer lives.

A Forward Deployed Engineer is an IBM engineer who works inside a client's environment, on the client's own systems and data and alongside the client's own people, to build and deliver an AI system that runs in production and is genuinely adopted. Deploy
Deploy moves the system into real use in controlled steps, shadow, then limited live, then full cutover, and measures whether the institution is actually adopting it.

What This Phase Means
A system that is live but unused has not gone live. This phase watches the true error rate and the real adoption rate at each step of rollout, and stabilizes drift, cost, and latency before they become incidents.

How the FDE Runs It
01
Run in shadow mode
The system produces output against live traffic, logged but not acted on, compared against the human decision. This surfaces the true error rate.
02
Move to limited live
One team, region, or slice of volume, with every human override logged and reviewed.
03
Cut over deliberately
Full cutover only after the override rate is stable and below threshold.
04
Track adoption from day one
Record users, frequency, and workarounds. Workarounds indicate the workflow was not redesigned to act on the output.
05
Stabilize
Monitor drift, cost, and latency in production and correct before they become failures.

The Field-to-Product Process
The field-to-product process is a repeatable system that simplifies the contribution process across the phases of feedback collection, design, and deploying the product for clients to use. The Three Stages

A client and a problem arrive. The first decision is whether this is an FDE engagement at all.
Selecting the Engagement
Engagement selection is how IBM decides which client opportunities receive a Forward Deployed Engineer. It runs on a project intake form that account teams complete for each opportunity, and an admin triage that reviews each submission against a consistent set of criteria. Because the FDE is IBM's most elite technical talent and that talent is limited, not every opportunity can or should get one. This section sets out how intake and triage work, what qualifies as an FDE engagement, the reasons an engagement is turned down, how opportunities are prioritized, and who makes the final decision.

The purpose is to point this capacity at the engagements where it changes the outcome, and to keep it from being absorbed into work that other teams are better suited to do. A clear selection process is what keeps the role from drifting into generic presales, routine implementation, or an escalation pool.

The Guardrail
An FDE is deployed against a scoped outcome and a checkpoint, never as staff augmentation
Every engagement enters on a named outcome and exits on a checkpoint. An FDE is not a discounted resource added to close a deal, and not open-ended capacity for a client's or account team's backlog. If the request is simply to lend an engineer, it is not an FDE engagement.

When an FDE Is the Wrong Choice
Because Forward Deployed Engineers are capable, they attract problems. Sales wants them to close deals, product wants them to explain gaps, customer success wants them to fix adoption, implementation wants them to handle complexity. Without discipline, the team becomes the most expensive place to send unresolved work. An FDE is the wrong choice in the following cases.

When to choose another team
01
The path is repeatable. If the client can adopt through standard onboarding, documentation, training, and configuration, an implementation or customer-success motion is better, cheaper, and more scalable.
02
The value is too low. Deploying an FDE against small configuration issues, routine support, or low-value custom requests teaches the organization to misuse elite technical talent.
03
The client will not participate. Building with the client requires access to users, data, systems, and decision-makers. If the client wants the vendor to disappear and return with a finished answer, forward deployment will struggle.
04
The work belongs to another team. When the problem is better served by IBM Consulting, Expert Labs, Support, or a partner, it should go there rather than to an FDE.
05
It would paper over a deeper gap. FDEs can reveal weak qualification, immature positioning, or broken processes, but they should not permanently absorb them. If every account needs an FDE to succeed, the issue is product, process, or market fit.
The Criteria and Who Decides
Qualified opportunities are scored on a common set of dimensions: strategic value, client commitment, technical complexity, production readiness, adoption potential, repeatability, product-learning potential, and delivery risk. The score does not decide on its own, but it puts every opportunity through the same lens. For complex or marginal cases, the FDE owns the final qualification, so the decision stays with the person accountable for the outcome.

Once an opportunity is qualified, the question turns from whether to take it to what it will take to deliver it. That is a question of skills.

The engagement is qualified. Now, what does it actually take to drive it to production and adoption?
The Skills the Work Demands
A qualified engagement has to be driven all the way to production and adoption. That takes a specific set of capabilities working together, and rarely a single person. IBM runs each engagement with a pod: a small, senior team assembled to cover four essential skill sets between them. Before forming the pod, it is worth being precise about what those four skill sets are and why each one is non-negotiable, because a missing capability is where engagements fail.

The Four Skill Sets
These are the capabilities every engagement must cover. They are skill sets, not headcount: a smaller or lower-risk engagement may see one FDE carry two of them, while a complex regulated account may need dedicated depth on each. What cannot happen is a skill set going missing.

01
The Problem-Finder
Gets past the stated problem to the one worth solving. Sits inside the client, maps how the work is really done, and frames the ambiguous ask into a scoped outcome with a measure of success. Without this, the pod builds the wrong thing well.
The Builder
02
Ships production-grade software in the client's environment, not prototypes. Stands up the agents and integrations, engineers for reliability from day one, and hardens the system until the client can trust it. This is the specialist for the pilot-to-production gap.
03
The Governance Lead
Makes the system trustworthy enough for the client's risk, compliance, and security functions to approve. Builds the evaluations with the client's experts, the monitoring, the guardrails, the model-risk and audit-ready artifacts. A different job from building: the builder asks does it produce the right output; this skill asks can the client's risk functions verify it will keep doing so, safely, under audit. In a regulated account this is often the longest part of the work and the thing that decides whether it reaches production at all.
04
The Engagement Lead
Owns the client outcome end to end, and owns the second job the whole model depends on: making sure what the pod learns and builds becomes product the next client inherits. Holds the sponsor, navigates the politics, drives adoption, and is explicitly accountable that field learning moves back to the product rather than being dropped when the team gets busy.
The Principle
These are skill sets a pod must cover, not always separate people
The value is in naming the capabilities, not in mandating four headcount. On one engagement a single FDE carries both problem-finding and engagement lead; on another the builder also carries governance. What cannot happen is a skill set going missing, because each one maps to a specific way engagements fail: the wrong problem, a build that cannot reach production, a system risk will not approve, or learning that never becomes product.

Why Each Skill Set Exists
Each capability is present because its absence is a known failure mode. This is the test for whether a pod is complete.

Problem-Finder
Absent, the pod builds a technically sound solution to the wrong problem.
Builder
Absent, the work stays a prototype and never survives real data in production.
Governance Lead
Absent, the system works in the demo but cannot clear the client's risk and compliance process.
Engagement Lead
Absent, the sponsor drifts, adoption stalls, and the learning never reaches the product.
These four capabilities are fixed. How they are distributed across FDEs is not. That is the pod-forming decision, and it is set by the problem in front of the team.

With the skills defined, the pod is assembled and sized to the problem in front of it.
Forming the Pod
With the four skill sets defined, the pod is assembled to run the engagement. The pod is small and senior, and its exact shape is a decision, not a template. What the pod looks like depends on what is being solved: all four skill sets must be covered, but how many FDEs cover them, and at what depth, is set by the problem.

The Pod Is Sized to the Problem
A pod is assembled by matching the four skill sets against the engagement in front of it. A narrow, low-risk use case with a clear sponsor might be run by two FDEs who each carry two skill sets. A complex, regulated, multi-workflow account might need dedicated depth on each. The engagement lead decides the composition, based on where the real difficulty sits.

Lean pod
A well-scoped, lower-risk use case with a committed sponsor. Two FDEs covering the four skill sets between them, often problem-finder with engagement lead, and builder with governance.
Standard pod
A typical production build. The builder and the engagement lead are distinct, with problem-finding and governance carried alongside as the phase demands.
Full pod
A complex or regulated account where governance is the long pole. Dedicated depth on each skill set, because no one FDE can carry two at the level the account requires.
What Decides the Shape
The composition follows the problem. Three questions set it.

How ambiguous is the problem?
A vague, unscoped ask needs dedicated problem-finding up front. A well-framed one lets that skill sit lighter.
How regulated is the client?
A bank or insurer makes governance the longest and most specialized part of the work, often its own person. A lower-risk environment lets the builder carry it.
How large is the build?
A single workflow can be built by one person. A multi-system, multi-workflow account needs more building capacity, and sometimes more than one builder.
What Stays Constant
Whatever the shape, two things never flex. All four skill sets are covered, and the pod is a named pairing accountable for an outcome, never bodies loaned to a client's team. The pod forms around a defined engagement with a scope and a transfer point, and when the work is done it exits.

The Rule That Protects the Pod
If the ask is lend us an engineer, it is not an FDE engagement
A pod is deployed against a named outcome with a defined finish, not added to a client's team as open-ended capacity. When the outcome is reached, day-two ownership passes to the client or its support partners and the pod exits. Any re-engagement is a new motion, scoped from the start, never a quiet continuation of the last one.

How the Product Side Works
The pod is Responsible at every stage, because the FDE brings the observation, designs the solution, and does the build. What moves is who is Accountable for the decision at each checkpoint. At Triage, Product Management owns the call on whether an item is accepted and where it belongs. At Validate, the Engineering Architect owns the technical approach and design approval. This is what keeps the field fast without compromising the product. One capability is accountable for the outcome; the rest are responsible for the work
A RACI where everyone is accountable is a RACI where no one is. The engagement lead owns the outcome so there is always a single answer to who is responsible for this engagement succeeding. The other three skill sets own their part of the work and hand cleanly between phases, which is what lets a small pod run a complex account without anything falling through.

The KPI Categories
The stage scorecard measures whether an engagement is moving. The KPI categories measure what it is worth. These are the six standard dimensions a forward-deployed practice is judged on. Every engagement is measured against all six, and together they answer the question leadership actually asks: did this engagement create value, and of what kind.

The Six Categories
Each category has a clear definition and a way it is measured. They span the full value of an engagement, from the revenue it influences to the reusable assets it leaves behind.

Revenue Influence
The commercial value the engagement moves: pipeline accelerated, deals closed, consumption committed. The FDE does not carry a quota, but the technical proof shortens the client's decision and unlocks spend.
Measured by: influenced pipeline and closed consumption tied to the engagement.
Time to Value
How quickly the client reaches a working, valuable result. The core promise of the model: closing the gap between a powerful product and a deployed outcome.
Measured by: days from engagement start to first production value, and to adopted production.
Adoption
Whether the institution actually uses what was built. A live system that no one uses has not delivered. Adoption is the truest test that the workflow was redesigned around the solution.
Measured by: active users, usage frequency, and the override rate on the system's output.
Expansion
Whether a successful engagement opens the next one: a second use case, a broader rollout, a renewal strengthened. This is how a lighthouse account compounds into a book of work.
Measured by: follow-on use cases and consumption growth in the account after handover.
Reusable Assets
What the engagement leaves behind for every future one: connectors, accelerators, reference implementations, and patterns published to the asset repository. This is the mechanism by which each engagement lowers the cost of the next.
Measured by: assets and patterns contributed, and their reuse rate on later engagements.
Customer Satisfaction
Whether the client would do it again and say so. The reference secured, the sponsor's confidence, the willingness to expand. The human confirmation that the outcome was real.
Measured by: satisfaction score, reference secured, and sponsor renewal intent.
How the Categories Relate to the Stages
The stage scorecard and the KPI categories measure different things at different times. The stage metrics are operational and real-time: they tell the pod whether the engagement is on track this week. The KPI categories are outcome-level: they accrue over the engagement and are read at handover and after. A checkpoint cleared is progress; a KPI met is value delivered.

The Distinction That Matters
Checkpoints measure motion; KPIs measure worth
An engagement can be clearing every checkpoint on schedule and still fail on the KPIs, if the thing being built well is not the thing that creates value. That is why both layers exist. The checkpoints keep the pod moving; the KPI categories keep it moving toward something worth reaching. The final page rolls both up to the portfolio level.

A Note on How to Use This
This rhythm is guidance, not a rigid standard. What matters is the intent behind it: steady delivery, captured learning, and a clear view of where every account sits. Adapt the cadence to the engagement; keep the intent.

The rhythm exists so that progress is continuous and visible, not so that meetings are held. If an engagement is shipping something material daily, confirming and capturing weekly, reviewing with its sponsor monthly, and feeding its learning into the product loop as it goes, it is running well, whatever the exact shape of its calendar. Adapt the cadence to the engagement. Hold to the intent: steady delivery, captured learning, and a portfolio you can see at a glance.

Everything in the model produces data. This is how it becomes the live dashboard the program runs on.
How the Dashboard Works
Everything in this operating model produces data, and that data is viewed, sliced and diced at the live portfolio dashboard. This will provide a single, operational view of the whole Americas FDE program. We model this in two ways: tactically and with a critical level of detail on a specific account, and strategically, looking across the cohort for trends, health and blockers for deployment and adoption of our AI solutions.

How the Data Feeds the Dashboard
The dashboard is an accumulated output of the operating rhythm running. Each layer of the model contributes a specific input.

The checkpoints
Each stage's checkpoints become the columns of the portfolio view. An account is always at exactly one point in the sequence, which is what makes every account comparable at a glance.
The scorecard
Each phase's leading and lagging metrics become the health read on an account: whether it is moving toward its next checkpoint or stalling before it. There are specific checkpoints for each stage that an account must pass through.
The RACI and rhythm
The weekly account reviews and portfolio stand-ups are where the data is confirmed and entered, so the dashboard stays current without a separate reporting burden. This is built into formal checkpoints with the FDE teams where conversations are recorded, and we use AI to map these into the health meter.
The product loop
Items moved into product and assets contributed become the program-level product-influence metrics, proving the second job is being done.
Two Views, Two Decisions
The dashboard is read at two altitudes, because two different people make two different kinds of decision from it.

Tactical
The Account Team
Drills into a single account: the journey so far, which checkpoints were crossed and how, the current stage's metrics against target, and any blocker. The decision it drives: why is this account stuck, and what do we do this week to move it.
Example: an account 71 days in governance review surfaces its stalled lagging metric, and the recommended decision to escalate to the client's model-risk sponsor.
Strategic
Program Leadership
Reads the whole portfolio by market and rolled up to Americas: how many accounts are engaged, the production conversion rate, where accounts are clustering, and which need a decision. The decision it drives: how is the program doing, where is it stuck at scale, and where should attention and capacity go.
Example: three accounts stuck at the same stage across two markets is a pattern to escalate, not three separate account problems.
What the Dashboard Shows
The portfolio view puts every engaged account on one surface, grouped by market and rolled up to Americas, each showing its stage, days in stage, and health. Accounts that need a decision are surfaced first. From any account, the view drills into its full journey: every checkpoint crossed with how and when, the live scorecard metrics, the value delivered, and, where an account is stuck, the specific reason and the recommended decision. The same checkpoints that define the model define the dashboard, so what leadership sees is exactly what the pods are running.

How a data team turns this model into the real dashboard: the record to capture, and how it flows.
The Data Model and Pipeline
The Dashboard Demo runs on synthetic data. To build the real one, a data team does not reverse-engineer the screens. It builds the record behind them. This appendix is that build brief. It defines the single points captured from every engagement, what is derived from those points, and the pipeline that carries the data from the intake surface to the live view.

What We Capture, Per Account
The dashboard runs on one record per account. This is the whole schema · exactly what to capture and how often. Nothing else is entered by hand.

Field	What It Is	Frequency

Market	One of the six Americas markets, plus sub-market	At intake
Industry	The client's sector	At intake
Motion	Pre-sales proof or post-sale activation	At intake
Use case	The agent or agents being built	At intake
Team roster	The seven named account roles	At intake
Sponsors	Executive and technical sponsor, name and title	At intake
CRM link	Opportunity id, carrying sales stage, win probability, and committed ACV or pipeline value	At intake, refreshed from CRM
Start date	When the engagement began	At intake
Target go-live	The committed production date	At intake
Engagement shape	The six factors · ambiguity, scope, integration, governance, data, change · each rated 0 to 2	At Scope
Current stage	Where the account sits in the five-stage run	Weekly
Checkpoints crossed	Which of the 18 checkpoints are cleared, with dates	Weekly
Scorecard readings	The current stage's leading and lagging metrics (each listed below with its formula and source)	Weekly
KPI readings	The six KPI categories and their sub-metrics (each listed below with its formula and source)	Weekly

What the Dashboard Derives
Do not capture these. They are computed from the fields above, so they never go stale or disagree with the record.

Health · green, amber, red	From pace against its thresholds, plus any stalled metric
Production conversion	Accounts in production divided by accounts engaged, across the portfolio

Reuse rate	Reuse instances divided by patterns available, across the portfolio
Value at stake	Sum of account values, across the portfolio
